# Residential plumbing and drain services (United States, 1-5 truck owner-operated companies)

Researched 2026-09-21 by the industry-researcher agent. Feeds a fictional sample site; every fact below is real-sourced or labelled. Not legal, insurance or safety advice: licensing, insurance and safety wording needs owner/professional review before any client uses it.

**Source-reading honesty.** Web fetching was unreliable this session (many DNS resets, and Angi, HomeGuide, Forbes, ConsumerAffairs and the Red Cross returned 403 or nothing). Each source below is tagged:
- **READ** = I fetched the page and read it.
- **SEARCH-ONLY** = I only saw a search-result summary of the page (or of a topic). Treat as unconfirmed until opened by a person.

## 1. How the business makes money and what a customer buys

A residential plumbing company sells four things, in rough order of how often they are bought:
1. **Repairs and clearing** (clogged drains, running or leaking toilets, faucets, disposals, small leaks). Low ticket, high frequency.
2. **Replacements and installs** (water heaters, toilets, faucets, disposals, sump pumps). Higher ticket. Water heater replacement is the biggest single job most small firms do (installed cost ranges in section 2).
3. **Diagnostics** (sewer camera inspection, leak location, pre-purchase inspections). Often the step that turns into a bigger job.
4. **Emergency response** (burst pipes, sewage backups, no hot water). Priced at a premium (section 2).

Typical sequence, first contact to paid (synthesised from the company pricing pages and consumer guides in section 2, not a single source):
call or web form -> dispatcher gives an arrival window and (sometimes) states the service-call fee -> tech arrives, diagnoses -> tech gives a quote (flat-rate price from a "price book", or hourly/time-and-materials) -> customer approves -> work done (permit and inspection if required) -> invoice, service-call fee credited or not depending on company -> payment at completion.

Revenue levers for a small firm: service-call fee, labour (hourly or flat-rate), parts markup, replacement jobs, and memberships/maintenance plans (not researched; no source read).

**Sample-site assumption (label as such, not a real business):** an owner-operated firm with 1-5 trucks, one licensed master or contractor-licence holder, techs holding journeyman-level or trainee status per the state.

### After-hours and emergency
- Multipliers: weeknights about 1.5x, weekends 1.5-2x, holidays up to 3x (HomeGuide as reported by Yahoo Local, READ, dated 2025-10-31, which also quotes Angi). Housecall Pro: 1.5-3x, separate from any dispatch fee, and hourly $150-$300 (READ, 2026-09-16).
- Flat emergency/trip fee: $150-$250 standard, $200-$400 after-hours and holiday (search summary, SEARCH-ONLY); one company reports an average $225 trip fee (Yahoo Local, READ).

### Hourly versus flat-rate, and why each is distrusted
- **Flat rate / price book:** one price for the whole job, quoted before work, comparable across bidders. Vendors say it raises average ticket 20-30% versus hourly (Housecall Pro, READ; this is a claim from a software vendor selling price-book tools, not independent data). Distrust: customers cannot see how the number is built, and the company keeps the upside if the job goes quickly. Also weakens with unfamiliar book prices ("why $450 for a 20-minute fix?"). The "30% higher satisfaction" figure in search summaries came from an unnamed survey and is not usable.
- **Hourly / time and materials:** you pay for time and parts; can be cheap for small jobs. Distrust: Roto-Rooter's own page concedes the final price is known only after the work, and complicated jobs frequently run over the initial estimate (READ). The meter-running fear is real.
- **Parts markup:** a search summary claims 200-400% over retail (99calls-type source, SEARCH-ONLY, unnamed data). Do not repeat.
- Small firm advantage: a written, itemised flat quote that shows labour, parts and the fee credit answers both distrusts. Texas's plumbing board itself advises that an estimate be detailed enough for the consumer to understand the work and how payment is expected (https://tsbpe.texas.gov/consumer-information/, READ).

**Lifespan**
| Type | Range | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tank (gas) | 8-12 years | Fuse Service, https://fuseservice.com/how-long-do-water-heaters-last-real-lifespans-explained/ | company blog, READ |
| Tank (electric) | 10-15 years | same | READ |
| Tank (general) | 8-12 years | CR Plumbing DFW, https://www.crplumbingdfw.com/water-heater-repair-vs-replacement-how-to-decide-what-it-costs/ (updated 2026-09-17) | READ |
| Tank | 10-15 years; tankless "more than 20 years" | attributed to Energy.gov in search summaries | SEARCH-ONLY. I could not open the Energy.gov page (404 on the URLs I guessed). Verify on energy.gov before publishing a DOE attribution. |
| Tankless | 15-20+ years (Fuse Service, READ); Rheem says similar (SEARCH-ONLY) | | |

Gaps: the manufacturer and Energy.gov numbers were not read directly. Hard water and skipped flushing shorten life (Fuse Service, READ). Use a range with "typical" wording, not a promise.

**Repair-or-replace rules plumbers actually use** (all company blogs; they agree closely):
- **50% rule:** if the repair costs about half or more of a comparable new unit, replace (Fuse Service, READ; several others SEARCH-ONLY). Same shape as the appliance rule already used in this studio's appliance demo.
- **Age gate:** CR Plumbing (DFW, READ): repair only if under about 6 years and the fix is under half of replacement cost; replace if past 10 years or showing rust, corrosion or repeated breakdowns. Search summaries add a looser "over 8 years and over 50%" version.
- **Tank leak:** a leaking tank (as opposed to a leaking fitting) generally means replace (implied by "rust, corrosion" rule above; no single source stated it as a rule).
- **Small fixes that usually justify repair:** thermostat, element, valve (CR Plumbing, READ).
- **Tankless adds cost drivers:** gas line upsizing and manufacturer venting (Permit Sonoma, READ).
- Sample-site framing (assumption): ask for both numbers on the same visit; publish the rule and both age and cost tests, as the appliance demo did.

## 4. Regulation and credentials

### What is verified
- **Licensing is state-by-state, with different structures.** California licenses at the contractor level (C-36 plumbing classification) with a **$25,000 contractor licence bond** since 2023-01-01, filed for the benefit of consumers harmed by defective work or licence-law violations and unpaid employees (CSLB bond page, https://www.cslb.ca.gov/contractors/maintain_license/bond_information/bond_requirements.aspx, READ). California does not use state journeyman/master titles (search summaries, SEARCH-ONLY).
- **Texas** uses a Responsible Master Plumber. The state board says the RMP licence number (starting "M") must appear in advertisements, and on written estimates and invoices along with the board's name, address and phone; consumers should check the database and that a certificate of insurance is on file (TSBPE, https://tsbpe.texas.gov/consumer-information/, READ). So on a Texas site the licence number is a legal display requirement, not just a trust badge.
- **Florida** requires licensed contractors to show their licence number on advertisements, bids and proposals, under Chapter 489 (search summary, SEARCH-ONLY; DBPR pages did not load). Verify at myfloridalicense.com.
- **Other states:** common summary is "about 45 states license plumbers at state level, the rest locally," with an apprentice, journeyman, master ladder taking roughly 4-5 years / 8,000-10,000 hours (search summaries of ServiceTitan, Next Insurance and others, **SEARCH-ONLY, and the count of 45 is unverified**). Master (or equivalent) generally supervises, pulls permits and runs the business.
- **Bond vs insurance are different things:** a licence bond protects the consumer or state and the company must reimburse the surety for any payout; general liability insurance protects the company from third-party claims (Insureon, Procore and others, SEARCH-ONLY, consistent with CSLB wording above). Requirements for each vary by state.
- **Permits:** replacing a water heater needs a permit and inspection in most cities and counties; gas heaters may need a separate gas/mechanical permit; some jurisdictions exempt like-for-like swaps, and there is variation (search summaries of several permit sites, SEARCH-ONLY). A California county example (Permit Sonoma, READ) requires a permit to replace a water heater and inspects seismic bracing, pressure-relief valve and drain, pipe insulation, gas-line sizing, venting, combustion air and expansion tank. So for like-for-like, the safe wording is "we pull permits where your city requires them", not "permits are always required".
- **Advertising law:** the FTC states advertisers must have evidence for claims and that advertising must be truthful (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business, READ). The FTC's Guides Against Bait Advertising exist at 16 CFR Part 238 (listed on ftc.gov, READ; text not read). State attorneys general enforce under state consumer-protection statutes (search summary).

### What a site can and cannot claim
| Claim | Rule for the sample site |
|---|---|
| "Licensed" | Only with a real licence number, class and state, shown where the state requires (Texas, Florida). For the fictional sample, use a clearly-marked placeholder such as "[Licence no. from client]" rather than a number that looks real. Never a plausible-looking fake number. |
| "Insured" | Only if the client holds a current general liability policy; say "general liability" and let the client supply the limit. Do not invent limits. Texas's board separately checks that a certificate of insurance is on file for master plumbers. |
| "Bonded" | Only if a bond exists. Meaning depends on the state; do not use it as a synonym for insured. California has a $25,000 licence bond. Some states require none. |
| "Licensed and insured" as one badge | Do not use as a decoration without a verifiable licence number and a stated state. Consumers are told to verify (Texas board, CSLB). |
| "Master plumber" / "journeyman" | Only for an individual holding that credential in that state; California has no such state titles. |
| "Gas work / gas fitting" | Only where the technician holds the gas licence or endorsement the state or city requires (see section 7). |
| "Free estimates" | Only if genuinely free. Diagnostics are often a charged service-call fee. Say which. |
| "24/7", "same-day", "arrives in 30 minutes" | Only if the client can staff it. |
| "Lowest price", "cheapest", "no hidden fees" | Avoid. Unprovable. Roto-Rooter's "no hidden or extra charges" for holidays, nights and weekends is a strong claim that the company has to be able to back (READ; see section 6). |
| "Certified" / "EPA certified" | Not relevant to ordinary plumbing (that is HVAC refrigerant); do not import from the appliance demo. |
| Health claims on water ("safe drinking water", "removes lead") | Need professional review; excluded. |
| Warranty | Only a warranty the client will honour in writing, with terms stated. |

**Needs owner or professional review:** all licensing, bonding, insurance and permit wording per state; any safety instruction; any warranty.

## 5. Emergency guidance safe to publish

Keep it short, link to authorities, tell the reader to call professionals, and never give repair instructions for gas.

### What counts as a plumbing emergency
No authority defines it. Common company usage (SEARCH-ONLY): burst pipe or uncontrolled leak, sewage backup, no water, no hot water in cold weather. Suggested site wording (assumption): "Water you cannot stop, sewage coming back up, or any smell of gas. If you can stop the water at the shutoff, it can usually wait for the next available appointment." That last clause is a sales-honest way to reduce needless after-hours premium calls; owner should agree.

### Shutting off the main water valve
Regional Water Providers Consortium (Portland area utilities, READ, https://www.regionalh2o.org/emergency-preparedness/emergency-water-shut): the valve is typically in the basement, garage, crawlspace, under the house or outside near the foundation; some homes lack one. Gate valve (wheel): turn clockwise until snug, possibly several turns. Ball valve (lever): turn until perpendicular to the pipe. Street-side or meter shutoff access depends on the water provider, so ask them before an emergency. After shutting off, open the faucets and flush toilets to relieve pressure, then contact a plumber and your insurer for interior pipes. The Red Cross and FEMA are cited for shutting off water at the main when pipes are damaged, but I only saw that in a search summary (SEARCH-ONLY). Electrical caution: keep away from standing water near outlets or panels (search summary of a plumber page; treat as a suggestion, and cite an electrical-safety authority if used).

### Gas smell
Wisconsin Public Service Commission (state regulator, READ, https://psc.wi.gov/Pages/ServiceType/Energy/PipelineSafety/NaturalGasConsumerSafety.aspx) and the New Hampshire Department of Energy (READ only via a fragment, https://www.energy.nh.gov/enforcement/pipeline-safety/what-do-if-i-smell-gas) and PHMSA (SEARCH-ONLY; page returned 403/DNS): leave the area right away, call the gas utility's emergency number or 911 from a safe distance outdoors; do not use phones, light switches, appliances, vehicles or flames near the leak; do not look for the source. Publish this as "leave, then call 911 or your gas utility from outside," and state plainly that a plumber is not the first call and that no one should attempt a DIY repair.

**Sources disagree on one detail:** an energy retailer (Constellation, READ) tells people to open doors and windows on the way out; other guidance says not to spend time on that or says not to open windows. Because sources conflict and the safest instruction is the shortest, **do not publish a "open windows" instruction.** Also skip carbon monoxide advice, which I did not research.

### Frozen pipes
American Red Cross (403 on fetch; SEARCH-ONLY): keep the heat on and let a trickle run in cold snaps; thaw with an electric heating pad, hair dryer, space heater or hot towels; never use a blowtorch, kerosene or propane heater, charcoal stove or any open flame; keep applying heat until pressure returns and check other faucets; call a licensed plumber if you cannot find, reach or thaw the frozen section. Recheck against the Red Cross page before publishing.

## 6. Customer objections, and how competitors' sites handle or hide them

### Objections and questions (before contact)
1. "How much will this cost, and will the number change once you are in my house?" Bait-and-switch on low advertised specials is one of the most often-cited complaints (Today's Homeowner, READ, 2024-04-04; Master Plumbers, search summary; consumer guidance also flags scare tactics and pressure to decide on the spot).
2. "Is the trip fee credited or is it in addition?" (section 2).
3. "Will you upsell a replacement when a repair would do?" Repeat complaint (search summary, SEARCH-ONLY).
4. "When exactly will you arrive, and will you call?" Arrival windows and no-shows: only search summaries mention it (SEARCH-ONLY); I found no primary data on prevalence.
5. "Who is coming into my home?" Licence, insurer, employee-or-subcontractor. One READ source lists unannounced subcontractors at inflated rates as a scam pattern, and cash-only, big deposits and avoiding credentials as red flags (Today's Homeowner, READ). Its "most states cap deposits at 10%" claim is unverified and state-specific; do not repeat.
6. "Are you really licensed?" Regulators tell consumers to check (TSBPE, READ; CSLB).
7. "Do I need a permit, and who pulls it?" (section 4).
8. "Is it an emergency?" (section 5).
9. "Do you warrant the work, and for how long?" (no sourced norm; ask the client).

### Competitor pages I actually viewed (paraphrased)
- **Roto-Rooter home page** (https://www.rotorooter.com/, READ 2026-09-21): no dollar prices on the first screen; promises "no hidden or extra charges" for holidays, nights and weekends; refers to a "more than a guarantee" without stating terms; no licensing or insurance statement on that page; customer reviews section showed as loading, i.e. no visible reviews in the fetched content; "over 90 years" and "since 1935" claims. Its /coupons/ page showed only a button, no offer terms in the fetched text (https://www.rotorooter.com/coupons/, READ). Its flat-rate-versus-hourly blog says the trip is free in most markets but franchises may charge, so the fee is not stated on the corporate site (https://www.rotorooter.com/blog/plumbing/flat-rate-pricing-vs-hourly-pricing/, READ). Note: a franchised national chain; the small-firm advantage is simply printing the number.
- **Omaha Drain** (https://www.omaha-drain.com/coupons, READ): a "$93 Main Drain Clearing" for a standard cable attempt in typical homes, with extra fees for difficult access or roof work; "$49 off" first service for new customers with a minimum service charge still applying; discounts need the coupon phrase said when booking; a free camera inspection only with qualifying drain work over $490. The $93 special is small; the conditions carry the meaning.
- **Simply Sewers Denver** (https://www.simplysewersdenver.com/specials-coupons/, READ): drain cleanout "starting at $99", camera "$149 (regular $199)", "free estimate", and every offer "not valid with other offers". "Starting at" and stacked exclusions are the classic pattern; the real range for a clog is not stated.
- **Paul Bunyan Plumbing** (https://paulbunyanplumbing.com/coupons/, READ): 16 "limited time" offers, all with "click for full details", including "Main line drain cleaning $99 off", a "$29 water heater tune and flush", a "$1,795 water heater installation, 50-gallon tall atmospheric", and a free service call "valued at $89" with a repair purchase. A perpetual "limited time" banner is manufactured urgency; the $89 fee here differs from the $100-$250 average on the same company's own blog (https://paulbunyanplumbing.com/blog/average-plumbing-repair-costs/, READ), so the two pages do not agree on what a call costs.
- What the four sites commonly leave out or blur: the fee amount and credit rule on the first screen; the actual range for the common job behind a "starting at" price; the conditions on the special (in fine print or "click for details"); the licence number and insurer; and how they price after hours.

I did **not** find, on the pages I read, any fabricated review scores. Roto-Rooter's reviews were not visible; I cannot judge others. The reports of fake reviews/licence numbers on competitor sites in the wider industry are not something I viewed, so I make no claim about them.

**Bait-and-switch patterns (documented in consumer material, SEARCH-ONLY unless marked):** a very low "special" price used to get inside the door, followed by a claim that the problem is bigger; scare talk; urging a quick decision; no written estimate. The FTC has a Guides Against Bait Advertising rule (16 CFR Part 238, existence READ on ftc.gov, content not read). The state attorney general is the usual enforcer of misleading price advertising (search summary).

## 7. What a small plumbing company typically does NOT do, or refers out

All SEARCH-ONLY (Angi gas fitter article, Eagle Eye and John Shelton excavation pages and a mold remediation vendor page appeared in search; none loaded). Confirm with the client and state rules.
- **Sewer line excavation or heavy trenching:** some plumbing firms have excavation crews and equipment; many refer it to excavation contractors, and it needs specialised permits and operators. State the sample firm's position explicitly. Trenchless work is another specialty (not researched).
- **Gas line installation and gas appliance hookups:** need a gas licence or endorsement where the state or city requires one; a plumber licensed for water and sewer only may not legally do it, so it is referred to a licensed gas fitter. State-by-state rule not confirmed. The sample firm should list gas work as "only with the correct licence, otherwise referred," and never suggest gas advice via the site.
- **Mold, and water-damage clean-up and restoration:** refer to remediation firms; plumbers stop the source. Remediation vendors say untrained disturbance spreads spores (a vendor page, SEARCH-ONLY; not an authority).
- **Also typically referred (assumption, no source read):** electrical, HVAC, septic tank pumping and drain-field work, well pumps, water treatment health testing, and structural repairs.
- **Cross-reference:** the appliance demo already excludes new gas line runs; keep the wording aligned.

## 8. Page implications

**First screen must answer (in this order):**
1. What we do and where: residential plumbing and drains, named towns or counties. Fictional sample.
2. How to reach a person now: one phone number (`tel:`), and a text option (`sms:`) only if the client will actually answer texts. No fake form success.
3. What a visit costs before work: service-call fee amount and the credit rule stated in plain words.
4. Emergency: a short, honest line: "Water you cannot stop? Shut the main valve (how to), then call us. Gas smell? Leave and call 911 or your gas utility."
5. Licence: state, class and number as the client supplies them, plus insurance in words. In a sample, mark placeholders.

**Exclusions (do not include):**
- No fake stats, "10,000 happy customers", invented review scores, testimonial carousels, fake "as seen on" or association logos.
- No "starting at $__" special without its real conditions shown next to it. No perpetual "limited time" banners.
- No "lowest price guarantee" or "no hidden fees" claim; write the actual terms instead.
- No copy that looks like a real licence number; no promise of arrival in X minutes.
- No stock photos of plumbers with wrenches; a uniform and van image implies a real person, so do not use one unless it is real. (Consistent with the studio's other demos, which use CSS-only graphics.)
- No health claims about water quality.
- No blog-farm content.

**Belongs in a table:**
- Price ranges by job, with a "what is included" column, a "range" column and a plain-language "why it varies" column. Label as sample-illustrative and cite ranges from section 2; do not present a range as a firm quote.
- After-hours schedule: weekday day / weeknight / weekend / holiday, with the multiplier (1.5x, 2x, up to 3x are sourced ranges; the sample firm's real multipliers are owner-set).
- Water heater repair-or-replace: age, cost ratio, signs, outcome.
- Services offered versus referred (section 7).
- What is credited: trip fee credit rule, permit fees (who pays), warranty terms.
- Licence and insurance facts: licence number, state board link to verify, insurer named by the client.

**Belongs in a list or short prose:** the arrival process (call, arrival window, diagnosis, written quote, approval, work, payment); the emergency steps; questions to ask any plumber, which builds trust more than adjectives.

**Sample-site suggested demo facts (assumption; not real prices):** fee value inside the sourced $75-$150 band, credited if the work is approved on the same visit; the site must label the number "sample".

## 9. Confidence and gaps

**Reasonably well supported (three or more independent sources agree):** trip fee band of roughly $75-$150 (up to $250) with credit-on-approval commonly offered but not universal; after-hours multipliers of about 1.5-3x; tank heater life about 8-15 years depending on type; repair-versus-replace 50% rule with an age gate; licensing varies by state and has a contractor-level model (California) and a master-plumber model (Texas); permits usually apply to water heater installs; gas-leak response is "leave and call from outside."

**Weaker (two sources or lead-gen / company blogs only):** ranges for jobs (all from lead-gen or company blogs); tankless life.

**Not confirmed:**
- Direct Energy.gov and manufacturer lifespan pages: not opened.
- The number of states licensing at state level ("about 45"): search summaries only.
- Florida licence-display rule text, Florida DBPR pages: not loaded.
- FTC bait-advertising text, PHMSA page, Red Cross frozen-pipe page, Angi pages (blocked), HomeGuide and ConsumerAffairs pages, permit code text (IPC/UPC).
- Insurance minimums, bond amounts and gas-licence rules by state other than California's bond and Texas's advertising rule.
- Whether a home-improvement or plumbing licence applies to specific small jobs below a dollar threshold (the California $1,000 unlicensed-work threshold is widely cited; the CSLB page returned a 404 and I did not confirm the current figure).
- Prevalence of no-shows or complaint statistics (no primary data found); the BBB/attorney general complaint volumes for plumbing.
- Any independent survey of what customers most fear; the objections list is assembled from consumer guides, not a survey.
- Membership/maintenance plan economics.
- Regional price differences except one company's statement that Minneapolis-St. Paul runs 20-30% above the national median.
- Competitor sites: only Roto-Rooter, Omaha Drain, Simply Sewers Denver and Paul Bunyan Plumbing were viewed. That is four sites, three of them regional; no truly small one-truck owner site was viewed. Independent 1-5 truck sites were not analysed.
- Simply Sewers page text differed from an earlier search summary ($99 drain and $149 camera on the fetched page versus $79 and $95 in the summary); I used the fetched text, and specials change.

**Needs the owner or a professional:** licensing/insurance/bond wording for any real client's state; any safety copy; warranty terms; any specific price.
